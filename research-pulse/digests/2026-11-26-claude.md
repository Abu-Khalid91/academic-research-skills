## 1. "The reference exists" is no longer a sufficient check (Oct 2026)
- *Real PMIDs, Wrong Papers* (arXiv 2610.29812, 21 Oct 2026). On 100 SciFact claims with live PubMed as the only tool, closed-book models cited PMIDs that almost always exist (327 of 335) but a cross-family judge rated only 3 of the 327 as relevant to the claim. For a search agent that wrote its own queries, 66–70% of cited PMIDs were relevant. With gold abstracts supplied, accuracy rose to 79–86% versus 62% for the best realistic setup, so retrieval, not reasoning, is the bottleneck.
- Limits stated by the author: pilot scale (N=100), two small models from one provider, one LLM relevance judge validated on 50 controls, no significant differences among the realistic setups.
- **So what:** this is direct outside support for the ARS design choice to separate the existence gate (v3.11) from the claim-faithfulness anchor layer (v3.7.3 L3). An existence-only checker would pass 98% of these citations. It is also a concrete, citable example for any pitch.

## 2. CiteAnchor: a stronger open citation checker, on the benchmark ARS planned to use (Oct 2026)
- *CiteAnchor* (arXiv 2610.30355, 21 Oct 2026; UW–Madison, Waterloo, Texas A&M). Field-by-field checks adapted to source type (paper, book, web), and a separate decision on whether the real source can be recovered. On the CiteAudit real-world test set (3,356 citations): F1 0.841, precision 0.875, recall 0.809 with a proprietary model, and F1 0.784 with an open-weight model. The CiteAudit baseline re-run on the same proprietary model scored 0.762. New benchmark CiteResolve: 423 real hallucinated citations labelled recoverable (252) or not (171); end-to-end success 74.7%.
- Useful details: 87% of citations needed no commercial search call. Replacing the staged checks with one LLM judgment over the same evidence cost 19.8 F1 points. Code is public. The paper notes that automated reference checks were used at ICLR 2026 and NeurIPS 2026 with flags passed to human reviewers.
- Stated limit: it checks bibliographic validity only, not whether the cited work supports the claim.
- **So what:** the number to beat on CiteAudit is now about 0.84 F1 at 0.875 precision. The open follow-up "run ARS against CiteAudit before any public precision claim" should use this as the comparator. Two ideas worth borrowing: the three-way field outcome (match, conflict, missing) and abstaining instead of proposing a merely plausible source.

## 3. Debate-collapse ledger paper, read in full — and a correction to the 11-23 digest (v2, 8 Nov 2026)
- *Measuring Collapse and Correction in Homogeneous-Panel LLM Debate* (arXiv 2609.35279v2, NeurIPS 2026 Evaluations and Datasets track). Confirmed figures: 6,925 MMLU-Pro debates, 253 collapses; 58.9% of collapses start in Round 1; the 8-probe screen correlates with collapse risk at ρ = 0.893 across 7 model families (p = 0.0123).
- **Correction:** the 11-23 digest said a freeze policy "lost 108 corrections to prevent 29 collapses". Those figures are from one illustrative replay in the paper's Figure 1. The headline result is larger: freezing every debate prevents 251 collapses but loses 804 corrections.
- Caveats the authors state: the screen is a triage signal, not a calibrated predictor (initial-majority accuracy is nearly as good, ρ = −0.821). Scope is same-model panels on closed-book multiple choice. It does not cover mixed-model panels, tool use, retrieval or free-text answers.
- **So what:** the four-cell table (preserved, collapse, correction, unrepaired) plus onset round is simple enough to adopt as-is for the AlSamadani Score dissent-collapse dimension. The scope limits are exactly the gap a clinical, free-text, mixed-model version would fill.

## 4. Clinical multi-agent teams: consensus hides bias, and more agents do not always help (Oct–Nov 2026)
- *Disparity Laundering* (arXiv 2610.38215, 28 Oct 2026; Drexel, Michigan). 120 MIMIC-IV emergency cases with only the race label swapped, 5 model families × 4 team structures. All 20 combinations kept the direction of the agents' first answers (more acute triage for Black patients). Fairness instructions and masking race words at the final step changed the gap by about 0.02 at most. In a worked case, debate removed the only dissenting answers, which were the ones pointing toward parity. Giving each agent a different demographic version of the patient cut the gap by 67–94%. Limits: one data source, same-model teams, descriptive estimates.
- According to PubMed, a JAMIA study on code-status extraction from 1,000 MIMIC-IV discharge summaries found that for capable models a single agent matched or beat reflective and extractor–reviewer designs, while multi-agent review doubled tokens and latency; it helped only the smallest model ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocag179)).
- According to PubMed, a JMIR systematic review of LLMs on emergency-department records (31 studies) found 71% were technical-performance-only, 4 had prospective deployment, and none measured patient outcomes ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2196/95400)).
- Nothing new found on LLM multi-agent systems specifically for sepsis or ICU bedside decisions in the last six weeks. New sepsis work is treatment-policy and forecasting models, not multi-agent debate (arXiv 2611.30951, 2611.13661, seen by abstract only).
- **So what:** "agreement" and "accuracy" both looked fine in the laundering study while the decision was biased. A dissent-collapse score that also records *which* dissent was removed would catch this; an agreement-only score would reward it. The JAMIA result is a caution for any claim that multi-agent review is a default safety gain.

## 5. AI peer reviewers and why models fabricate citations (Oct–Nov 2026)
- *Auditing AI peer reviewers* (arXiv 2611.17052, NeurIPS 2026; Cambridge, Flatiron). 357 errors injected into 30 real arXiv papers, 12 reviewer systems. With the same prompt, three frontier models differed by about 50 points in detection. Citation errors were the hardest type: at most 26.7% detected on astrophysics by any system that did not retrieve the cited paper, versus 46.7% for the one that did. Two fine-tuned open-weight reviewers detected under 3%. Adjudication was LLM-based with a human check on one paper.
- *Are Fake Citations a Form of Reward Hacking?* (arXiv 2611.01313, 31 Oct 2026, pre-registered, one author, open-weight models only). Asking for citations raised fabrication in post-trained models from 34.2% to 73.7% and lowered it in base models from 9.0% to 0.8%. A preference model trained on surface citation features preferred post-trained output 86% of the time, driven by citation count. The author's own verdict on reward hacking: "possibly, but not proven". Absolute rates rest on an LLM judge with 80% precision.
- *VerifyAgent* (arXiv 2611.27839, 20 Nov 2026, single author, 40 hypotheses, one local model): an advocate/skeptic/arbitrator verifier with a stopping rule. The honest reading is weak: hallucination 21.7% vs 25.9% for a single critic, p = 0.61.
- **So what:** the peer-review benchmark supports ARS's retrieval-backed citation checks over reviewer memory, and its open benchmark is a ready test for `academic-paper-reviewer`. The reward-hacking study is a reason to keep "cite only what was retrieved" rules in the writer agents.
